## Background

To start, the basic idea behind speculative decoding: decode is memory-bound,
so a target-model forward spends most of its time moving weights, and at small
batch verifying several tokens costs about the same as generating one. A small
drafter writes K tokens first, and the target checks them all in one forward.
The accepted prefix is committed, and the verify forward emits one bonus token
as a side effect. Rejected drafts only waste the drafter's cheap compute:

<SpecRaceViz client:visible lang="en" />

The trade sours as load grows. At batch size B with K speculative tokens the
target verifies B×K tokens every step. At small batch those extra tokens ride
along with the weight traffic almost for free. As the batch fills the GPU,
every verify token starts to cost real step time, and past a point verification
costs more than it saves. The diagram below prices the same three choices at
different loads:

<CostViz client:visible lang="en" />

The rest of this post is in three parts: the two algorithm generations that
attack this trade, the engineering SGLang builds to serve them, and what that
stack means for DeepSeek-V4.

## The algorithms: DFlash, then DSpark

### DFlash: parallel drafting with KV injection

Most speculative methods, including the EAGLE series and the native MTP heads
that ship with models like DeepSeek-V4, draft autoregressively: the small model
emits tokens one by one, so draft cost grows with draft length, and keeping
latency low forces the drafter to stay shallow.

DFlash, developed by Z Lab, replaces the autoregressive drafter with a
lightweight block diffusion model that emits the whole block in one forward.
Making that work is not just swapping the drafter. A small diffusion model
trained from scratch accepts poorly, and a full-size diffusion LLM is too
expensive to draft with. DFlash's answer is KV injection: the target model
knows the context best, so DFlash extracts the target's hidden states and
injects them into the draft model's KV cache at every draft layer. EAGLE-style
methods feed target features only at the drafter's input, and that signal fades
as the drafter gets deeper. Injected at every layer, the context signal stays
strong, and the drafter can skip modeling the context entirely and spend its
capacity on predicting the block:

<DFlashViz client:visible lang="en" />

The two pieces contribute separately, and the paper's ablation makes that
visible. Diffusion alone already beats EAGLE-3 end to end on speed, even at
lower acceptance. Injection alone raises acceptance above EAGLE-3. Together
they hold acceptance at EAGLE-3's level while drafting an order faster:
on GSM8K, 4.2 accepted tokens and 3.3× end-to-end against EAGLE-3's 4.2 and
2.1×. On Qwen3.5-397B-A17B, the jointly released drafter reaches 4.3× the
non-speculative baseline and 1.5× MTP at concurrency 1 on HumanEval:

<DFlashBenchViz client:visible lang="en" />

### DSpark: draft semi-autoregressively, verify on a budget

A purely parallel block leaves two things on the table. First, the block's
positions are drafted without intra-block dependencies, so quality decays
toward the tail of the block; the DSpark paper calls this suffix decay.
Second, the verify side is untouched: every request still pays for a
full-block verify, which is exactly the B×K cost from the Background at high
concurrency.

DSpark, from DeepSeek, addresses both ends with three draft-side pieces. The
block drafter still emits a gamma-token block in one forward, but a lightweight
sequential head (a Markov or RNN head) conditions each position on the previous
token; this is what semi-autoregressive means, and it is the piece that
counters suffix decay. The confidence head scores each drafted token's chance
of surviving verification, and the product across the block is the block's
survival probability. Sequential Temperature Scaling (STS) calibrates those
scores, because raw scores are systematically overconfident and any budget
built on them must reflect true acceptance rates. Those survival scores are
what makes the verify length schedulable per request: walk down the block's
survival curve and stop where the expected accepted tokens no longer cover the
marginal cost of verifying one more token. The diagram below follows one
request through a draft step:

<BlockDraftViz client:visible lang="en" />

Across Qwen3-4B, 8B, and 14B targets, the paper reports 16.3%, 18.4%, and
18.3% higher macro-average accepted length than DFlash. The rest is a serving
problem: someone has to price the marginal cost, cut the budgets every step,
and make a batch of unequal verify lengths run fast on real GPUs. That is the
SGLang part.

## How SGLang serves DSpark

SGLang integrated DSpark in
[#30261](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/pull/30261) (merged July 2026),
building the drafter directly on the DFlash draft model, for both dense and
sparse targets such as Qwen3 and DeepSeek-V4. Across a full concurrency sweep
on DeepSeek-V4-Flash, the integration holds the best throughput/latency
frontier, ahead of both MTP and the non-speculative floor:

The rest of this section walks through the engineering that turns the paper's
schedule into wall-clock time.

### The confidence scheduler and verify modes

SGLang's confidence scheduler runs the budget rule from the previous section
every step, for every request in the batch. A predictable request keeps a long
window. A divergent one gets trimmed to two or three tokens. Higher load raises
the marginal cost of every verify token, so all windows tighten together:

<TrimViz client:visible lang="en" />

Around the scheduler, SGLang ships three verify modes, and the rest of this
post keeps referring to them. static verifies the full drafted block every
step; it is the baseline. compact verifies only the window the scheduler
picked; it is the production path. cap-accept verifies the full block but
commits only up to the window, so it produces exactly compact's output while
also observing what a full verify would have accepted:

<VerifyModesViz client:visible lang="en" />

### Ragged verify under full CUDA graphs

Per-request windows do not fit a fixed-shape CUDA graph. A batch where one
request verifies two tokens and another six has no single query length, and
padding everyone back up to the block width just pads the trim back in. SGLang
keeps the batch ragged instead. The verify tokens are front-packed into one
compact buffer, and the graph is keyed on the total token count, rounded up to
the nearest captured tier. When budgets trim, the packed total drops to a
smaller tier, and the replayed graph is genuinely cheaper: fewer attention and
MLP rows, not a masked full-width forward. Under data-parallel attention the
ranks share one tier, the largest any rank needs, and step down together:

<PackViz client:visible lang="en" />

The packed buffer is a cu_seqlens-style varlen input, so the compact verify
reuses attention kernels the backend already has. On DeepSeek-V4 it runs the
model's own sparse-MLA path (flash_mla) with no new kernel. Each supported
backend just rebuilds its varlen metadata from the packed layout on graph
replay.

### The cost table behind the budgets

The scheduler prices verify tokens with the additive model from the Background
figure: T(bs, K) = bias + α(bs) + θ(M), with M = bs + K the step's total
tokens. α(bs) is the request-scaling floor, the draft pass plus part of
attention, and trimming cannot move it. θ(M) is the target's verify-token cost,
the only term trimming recovers, so trim headroom shows up only where θ is
large. SGLang profiles the table offline by sweeping a batch × verify-fraction
grid, and the scheduler reads it online, trading expected accepted tokens
against real marginal cost. The blog validates the model's predictions against
a live server.

### Scheduling results

How much does the dynamic budget actually buy? The blog compares compact
against no-trim, which runs the static full-block schedule through the same
ragged path, on two example workloads that differ in acceptance. The win is
primarily a high-batch effect. At batch size 1 the target verify barely slows
down with more tokens, so trimming saves little and the two arms tie. As
concurrency grows and throughput starts to plateau, trimming shortens the step
and compact pulls ahead. The gap is larger, and opens earlier, on the
lower-accept workload, because lower acceptance leaves more tail to trim.

The scheduler behind these numbers is a first, vanilla version, and the blog
treats it that way: a proof that the mechanism works end to end. The two
example workloads also differ slightly in setup beyond acceptance, so the
trend across them is the result, not absolute cross-panel numbers. And the
budgets are only as good as the cost tables behind them; the current fit is a
first approximation that may not fully account for context length.

Homogeneous sweeps also hide the real point of confidence scheduling: two
requests in the same batch should not get the same verify window if one is far
more predictable than the other. On a mix of gsm8k, arena-hard, and poetry,
the window contracts with acceptance difficulty, 5.24 to 3.78 to 2.91 tokens,
while utilization against the ceiling stays at 0.88–0.97. The per-step
distribution shows the same thing: about 55% of gsm8k steps fill the full
window of six, while about 80% of poetry steps use three or fewer:

### Zero-overhead scheduling and kernels

Two kinds of engineering turn the schedule into wall-clock time: cutting the
cost of each step, and hiding the scheduler behind the forward. Together they
reach 383.7 tok/s at accept length ~5 at batch size 1, on DeepSeek-V4-Pro with
TP8 on B300.

On the kernel side, SGLang rewrote the clusters of tiny ops around the verify
as fused Triton kernels: the compact scatter, the SWA page-index, the
verify-length top-k schedule, and the ragged-window packing. The block
drafter's sampling path folds into fused kernels, and its matrix multiplication
is sharded. In one example profile, the work outside the target verify shrinks
by 1.7 ms, against a 7.3 ms verify.

On the scheduling side, DSpark drops straight into SGLang's zero-overhead
(overlap) scheduler with almost no special-casing. The spec-v2 runtime was
introduced together with the DFlash integration, where its overlap scheduling
added another 33% throughput on top of the initial implementation. It overlaps
the next step's scheduling with the current forward on separate streams, and
DSpark joins as a first-class worker: forward outputs come back as async
futures, cross-iteration ordering rides the runtime's device-side barrier, and
on-device page tables mean no per-step host sync. The confidence relay uses
the same channel, read two steps back. The decode loop then runs with no
per-step bubble, about 1.5× tighter than with the overlap scheduler off:

### Observability

Trimming censors the very number you need to judge it. compact only verifies
the window, so how many tokens a full-block verify would have accepted at that
step is never observed, and without that ceiling you cannot tell a good trim
from a lossy one. A cap-accept run recovers the ceiling exactly: it verifies
the full block, commits only up to the window, and therefore matches compact's
output while measuring what trimming left behind.

For production runs where an extra companion arm is unwanted, a block-accept
estimator recovers an estimated ceiling directly inside a compact run. It uses
the target tokens of future steps with their logprobs, and computes estimation
intervals for the counterfactual tail. SGLang also surfaces per-request
confidence and calibration metrics, such as ECE, for post-hoc analysis.

## Serving DeepSeek-V4 with SGLang

DeepSeek-V4 ships in two sizes, the 1.6T Pro and the 284B Flash. Each layer
mixes sliding-window attention with compressed attention to keep the 1M-token
context tractable, and the checkpoint includes a natively trained single-layer
MTP head. SGLang has served the family since day 0, with prefix caching,
kernels, and parallelism built for the hybrid attention path; the day-0 blog in
the references covers that side.

Everything in this post sits on top of that as a serving-time choice.
Speculation never touches the target model's architecture or its output
distribution: the drafter is a separately trained companion model, and turning
speculation off just means loading the plain checkpoint. On the same V4
target, `--speculative-algorithm` selects the generation of drafter: EAGLE
runs the MTP head that ships inside the checkpoint, DFLASH runs a
block-diffusion drafter, and DSPARK runs the confidence-scheduled drafter that
DeepSeek releases as companion checkpoints (DeepSeek-V4-Flash-DSpark,
DeepSeek-V4-Pro-DSpark). The DSpark paper reports that in DeepSeek's own V4
serving system, under live user traffic, DSpark accelerates per-user
generation by 60–85% over the MTP baseline at matched throughput. Full launch
recipes and reproduction commands live in the original blogs in the
references.
