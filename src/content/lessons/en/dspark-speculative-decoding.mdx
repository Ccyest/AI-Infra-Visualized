## Background

To start, the basic idea behind speculative decoding: decode is memory-bound,
so a target-model forward spends most of its time moving weights, and at small
batch verifying several tokens costs about the same as generating one. A small
drafter writes K tokens first, and the target checks them all in one forward.
The accepted prefix is committed, and the verify forward emits one bonus token
as a side effect. Rejected drafts only waste the drafter's cheap compute:

<SpecRaceViz client:visible lang="en" />

The trade sours as load grows. At batch size B with K speculative tokens the
target verifies B×K tokens every step. At small batch those extra tokens ride
along with the weight traffic almost for free. As the batch fills the GPU,
every verify token starts to cost real step time, and past a point verification
costs more than it saves. The diagram below prices the same three choices at
different loads:

<CostViz client:visible lang="en" />

DSpark attacks both ends of this trade. A semi-autoregressive block drafter
emits a whole block per draft forward, so acceptance stays high. A confidence
head scores each drafted token, and the verify length becomes a per-request
budget that stops paying for tokens the workload is unlikely to accept. The
algorithm and its gains come from the DSpark paper; SGLang integrated it in
[#30261](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/pull/30261) (merged July 2026)
for both dense and sparse models, such as Qwen3 and DeepSeek-V4. Across a full
concurrency sweep on DeepSeek-V4-Flash, DSpark holds the best
throughput/latency frontier, ahead of both MTP and the non-speculative floor:

<FrontierViz client:visible lang="en" />

The rest of this post walks through the mechanism, and the engineering that
turns a verify schedule into wall-clock time.

## DSpark

### Design and mechanism

The draft side has three pieces. The block drafter emits a gamma-token block in
one forward, with a lightweight sequential head (a Markov or RNN head)
conditioning each position on the previous token, which is what
semi-autoregressive means here. The confidence head scores each drafted token's
chance of surviving verification, and the product across the block is the
block's survival probability. Sequential Temperature Scaling (STS) calibrates
those scores, because raw scores are systematically overconfident and the
scheduler budgets against true acceptance rates.

On top of the algorithm, SGLang adds a confidence scheduler. Each step it
converts per-block survival into a per-request verify budget: it walks down the
block's survival curve and stops where the expected accepted tokens no longer
cover the marginal cost of verifying one more token. The diagram below follows
one request through a draft step:

Across a batch, the same rule sizes every request separately. A predictable
request keeps a long window. A divergent one gets trimmed to two or three
tokens. Higher load raises the marginal cost of every verify token, so all
windows tighten together:

### Verify modes

SGLang ships three verify modes, and the rest of this post keeps referring to
them. static verifies the full drafted block every step; it is the baseline.
compact verifies only the window the scheduler picked; it is the production
path. cap-accept verifies the full block but commits only up to the window, so
it produces exactly compact's output while also observing what a full verify
would have accepted:

## Ragged verify under full CUDA graphs

Per-request windows do not fit a fixed-shape CUDA graph. A batch where one
request verifies two tokens and another six has no single query length, and
padding everyone back up to the block width just pads the trim back in. SGLang
keeps the batch ragged instead. The verify tokens are front-packed into one
compact buffer, and the graph is keyed on the total token count, rounded up to
the nearest captured tier. When budgets trim, the packed total drops to a
smaller tier, and the replayed graph is genuinely cheaper: fewer attention and
MLP rows, not a masked full-width forward. Under data-parallel attention the
ranks share one tier, the largest any rank needs, and step down together:

<PackViz client:visible lang="en" />

The packed buffer is a cu_seqlens-style varlen input, so the compact verify
reuses attention kernels the backend already has. On DeepSeek-V4 it runs the
model's own sparse-MLA path (flash_mla) with no new kernel. Each supported
backend just rebuilds its varlen metadata from the packed layout on graph
replay.

## Scheduling results

How much does the dynamic budget actually buy? The blog compares compact
against no-trim, which runs the static full-block schedule through the same
ragged path, on two example workloads that differ in acceptance. The win is
primarily a high-batch effect. At batch size 1 the target verify barely slows
down with more tokens, so trimming saves little and the two arms tie. As
concurrency grows and throughput starts to plateau, trimming shortens the step
and compact pulls ahead. The gap is larger, and opens earlier, on the
lower-accept workload, because lower acceptance leaves more tail to trim.

The scheduler behind these numbers is a first, vanilla version, and the blog
treats it that way: a proof that the mechanism works end to end. The two
example workloads also differ slightly in setup beyond acceptance, so the
trend across them is the result, not absolute cross-panel numbers. And the
budgets are only as good as the cost tables behind them; the current fit is a
first approximation that may not fully account for context length.

### Per-request differentiation on mixed traffic

Homogeneous sweeps hide the real point of confidence scheduling: two requests
in the same batch should not get the same verify window if one is far more
predictable than the other. On a mix of gsm8k, arena-hard, and poetry, the
window contracts with acceptance difficulty, 5.24 to 3.78 to 2.91 tokens, while
utilization against the ceiling stays at 0.88–0.97. The per-step distribution
shows the same thing: about 55% of gsm8k steps fill the full window of six,
while about 80% of poetry steps use three or fewer:

### The cost table behind the budgets

The scheduler prices verify tokens with the additive model from the Background
figure: T(bs, K) = bias + α(bs) + θ(M), with M = bs + K the step's total
tokens. α(bs) is the request-scaling floor, the draft pass plus part of
attention, and trimming cannot move it. θ(M) is the target's verify-token cost,
the only term trimming recovers, so trim headroom shows up only where θ is
large. The table is profiled offline by sweeping a batch × verify-fraction
grid, and the scheduler reads it online, trading expected accepted tokens
against real marginal cost. The blog validates the model's predictions against
a live server.

## Zero-overhead scheduling and kernels

Two kinds of engineering turn the schedule into wall-clock time: cutting the
cost of each step, and hiding the scheduler behind the forward. Together they
reach 383.7 tok/s at accept length ~5 at batch size 1, on DeepSeek-V4-Pro with
TP8 on B300.

On the kernel side, SGLang rewrote the clusters of tiny ops around the verify
as fused Triton kernels: the compact scatter, the SWA page-index, the
verify-length top-k schedule, and the ragged-window packing. The block
drafter's sampling path folds into fused kernels, and its matrix multiplication
is sharded. In one example profile, the work outside the target verify shrinks
by 1.7 ms, against a 7.3 ms verify.

On the scheduling side, DSpark drops straight into SGLang's zero-overhead
(overlap) scheduler with almost no special-casing. The spec-v2 runtime already
overlaps the next step's scheduling with the current forward on separate
streams, and DSpark joins as a first-class worker: forward outputs come back as
async futures, cross-iteration ordering rides the runtime's device-side
barrier, and on-device page tables mean no per-step host sync. The confidence
relay uses the same channel, read two steps back. The decode loop then runs
with no per-step bubble, about 1.5× tighter than with the overlap scheduler
off:

## Observability

Trimming censors the very number you need to judge it. compact only verifies
the window, so how many tokens a full-block verify would have accepted at that
step is never observed, and without that ceiling you cannot tell a good trim
from a lossy one. A cap-accept run recovers the ceiling exactly: it verifies
the full block, commits only up to the window, and therefore matches compact's
output while measuring what trimming left behind.

For production runs where an extra companion arm is unwanted, a block-accept
estimator recovers an estimated ceiling directly inside a compact run. It uses
the target tokens of future steps with their logprobs, and computes estimation
intervals for the counterfactual tail. SGLang also surfaces per-request
confidence and calibration metrics, such as ECE, for post-hoc analysis.

## What's next

The roadmap lives in
