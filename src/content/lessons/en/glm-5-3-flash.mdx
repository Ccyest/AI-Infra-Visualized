## Background

GLM-5.3-Flash is the first natively multimodal model in the GLM-5 series: 320B total
parameters, 18B active per token, a native 1M-token context, released under the MIT
license. Before the release, Z.ai ran it anonymously as ox-alpha on OpenCode and
OpenRouter for about a week. It became the most-used model on OpenRouter in that
window, at 23.2T tokens over six days, and all of that traffic was served on Chinese
AI chips.

The design goal is cost. Compared with the GLM-4.5 series, the total parameter count
is similar (320B vs. 355B), but the active parameters nearly halve (18B vs. 32B) and
the layer count halves (45 vs. 92). Z.ai reports that it outperforms GLM-5.2 across
benchmarks at one-tenth the price. The rest of this lesson walks through where the
cut comes from: the attention stack, the indexer on top of it, and the residual
connections.

## The layer stack

<ArchStackViz client:visible lang="en" />

The 45 layers interleave two attention types at a 3:1 ratio: 34 layers use KDA
linear attention and 11 layers use DSA sparse attention. Every fourth layer is DSA,
and the final layer is KDA. The first 3 layers use a dense FFN; the other 42 use MoE
with 288 routed experts plus 1 shared expert, of which 8 routed experts are active
per token. One MTP layer sits on top for speculative decoding. Images enter through
a 24-layer ViT and join the same stack, which is what "natively multimodal" means
here: one stack serves text and vision.

Two structural choices are easy to miss in the diagram. First, main attention uses
no RoPE: `qk_rope_head_dim` is 0 in the config, and position comes from the KDA
recurrence. Kimi K3 made the same choice, and the
[Kimi K3 lesson](https://yichizhang.dev/AI-Infra-Visualized/en/lessons/kimi-k3-day0-support/)
covers why input-dependent decay can replace explicit position encoding. Second,
residuals are not plain additions: every sublayer output passes through mHC, which
gets its own section below.

## Hybrid attention

### KDA layers

KDA is a linear-attention variant. Instead of appending one KV entry per token, each
head keeps a fixed 128×128 state matrix. Every new token updates that state in
place: a per-channel gate decays old content, then a delta-rule write corrects the
state along the current key. The state never grows. With 64 heads per layer, one
request holds about 4MB per KDA layer in BF16, roughly 140MB across all 34 KDA
layers, whether the context is 1K or 1M tokens. The full mechanism walkthrough, from
MHA to DeltaNet to KDA, is in the
[Kimi K3 lesson](https://yichizhang.dev/AI-Infra-Visualized/en/lessons/kimi-k3-day0-support/);
GLM-5.3-Flash even keeps the same decay lower bound (`gate_lower_bound = -5`).

A fixed-size state cannot keep every token apart, so precise long-range retrieval
still needs layers that read history per position. Kimi K3 keeps full global MLA in
24 of its 93 layers for that job. GLM-5.3-Flash also keeps 11 global layers, but
they are not full attention.

### DSA layers

The global layers use DSA, the sparse attention mechanism from DeepSeek-V3.2. Each
DSA layer is MLA-based: every token stores one 512-dim compressed latent (1KB in
BF16) instead of per-head K and V. On top sits a lightning indexer with 32 heads of
128 dims. At each decode step, the indexer scores the history, top-k selection keeps
the best-scoring entries (`index_topk = 2048`), and main attention reads only those.
Once the context grows past the top-k budget, reads per step stop growing: compute
is capped, while the full KV history stays addressable.

### IndexPool

At a 1M-token context, the indexer scan is the remaining term that grows with
context: one score per history token per step, and a 1M-entry indexer cache. Here
GLM-5.3-Flash adds its own piece, IndexPool. Every 4 indexer keys are compressed
into 1 cache entry through a learned weighted sum, so the cache and the per-step
scoring both shrink 4×. Selection then works at 4-token blocks, and the tail block
is always selected regardless of score, so the newest tokens are never dropped.

### Cost curves

The blog's own comparison shows the division of labor. Kimi K3 pairs KDA with full
MLA, so its global layers rescan the whole history at every decode step, and its
per-token attention compute is the highest line in the chart. GLM-5.3 uses DSA in
every layer, so its compute is capped, but every layer still stores KV, and its
cache is the steepest line. GLM-5.3-Flash cuts both at once: KDA removes the KV from
34 layers, and DSA caps the reads in the other 11.

<CostCurvesViz client:visible lang="en" />

In absolute terms, the 11 DSA layers store about 11KB of KV latents per token in
BF16, so a full 1M-token request holds about 11GB before any cache quantization,
plus the pooled indexer cache. The blog is explicit that this still lands slightly
above Kimi-K3 and DeepSeek-V4-Flash on KV size, and calls it room for improvement.

## mHC

Hyper-Connections widen the residual stream. Instead of one running sum, the model
keeps `hc_mult = 4` parallel streams, and a learned 4×4 matrix mixes them around
every sublayer. The catch is the identity mapping. A plain residual passes a signal
through unchanged, and that property is what keeps very deep networks trainable.
Once a learned matrix sits on the path, each crossing multiplies the signal by that
matrix. If its row and column sums drift from 1, the drift compounds across 45
layers of repeated crossings, and training becomes unstable at scale.

mHC, from DeepSeek, restores the property by constraint. Each mixing matrix is
projected to be doubly stochastic: all entries non-negative, every row and every
column summing to 1. The projection runs inside the forward pass as Sinkhorn-Knopp
iterations, which alternately normalize rows and columns; the config runs 20
(`hc_sinkhorn_iters = 20`). A doubly stochastic matrix can only average streams,
never amplify them, so identity mapping comes back while the extra width and the
learned routing between streams stay.

## Results

The release benchmarks put the efficiency story next to capability. Selected
numbers from the blog:

| Benchmark | GLM-5.3-Flash | GLM-5.2 | Claude Opus 4.8 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Terminal Bench 2.1 | 84.3 | 81.0 | 85.0 |
| DeepSWE v1.1 | 63.4 | 46.2 | 58.0 |
| AutomationBench v1.0.6 | 48.8 | 26.2 | 41.0 |
| Toolathlon Verified | 78.4 | 59.9 | 76.2 |

On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v4.1.1, GLM-5.3-Flash scores 57 at
$0.045 per task (discounted), which places it on the cost-intelligence Pareto
frontier of that index.

## Serving on SGLang

SGLang opened day-0 support at release:
[sglang#36507](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/pull/36507) carries the model
support, and the
[GLM-5.3-Flash cookbook](https://docs.sglang.io/cookbook/autoregressive/GLM/GLM-5.3-Flash)
merged the same day, with FP8 KV cache and the TRT-LLM DSA attention backend as the
default recipe on Blackwell GPUs.

The production tie runs deeper than day-0 support. Z.ai writes that the inference
engine serving this model on Chinese AI chips was built on top of SGLang, with an
Encode–Prefill–Decode (EPD) disaggregated architecture that scales multimodal
encoding, prefill, and decoding as independent worker pools, plus W8A8 weight
quantization and hybrid INT8/FP8/BF16 cache quantization. Against their initial
baseline on the same hardware, that stack reached a 3× end-to-end serving
improvement, at per-token costs comparable to mainstream NVIDIA GPUs.
